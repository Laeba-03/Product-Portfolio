Coach Plan Modification: giving coaches the final say on AI training plans

How weekly AI-generated plans reach run coaches at RunPlayGo, and how coaches review, change and approve them. Shipped in 30 days.

A coach reviews the week

Illustration of the review flow, not product data.
    At a glanceStatus: shipped
    Scale: ~50 coaches onboarded
    Timeline: 30 days to ship
    Team: 5, including developers
    My role: Assistant Product Manager, reporting to the Head of Product. I ran the coach research, defined the review and edit flow, wrote the PRD and prioritized the scope.

    The context

    RunPlayGo is a coach-led running platform in India. Runners choose a goal, find a coach and subscribe. Coaches use a separate coach app, which I own, for plans, run history and stats, feedback, chat and scheduling for online and offline sessions. The platform also brings coaches new trainees alongside their existing ones.

    The problem: coaches had no platform, so growth stopped at a few trainees

    Plans, trainee feedback and chats were scattered across WhatsApp, Excel sheets and notebooks, often managed alongside another job, with trainees able to call at any time. Plans also go stale fast: a missed session, a hard week or an injury risk means the plan has to change that day, and without run data in front of them coaches adjusted from what trainees told them, not what they ran. With no single tool or process, coaches couldn't take on more trainees even when they wanted to.

    I spoke with up to 30 coaches about how they work before designing anything.

    The decision: AI drafts, the coach decides

    I weighed three options:

    • Fully automated: AI generates and adjusts plans and trainees receive them directly
    • Fully manual: coaches build everything with no AI
    • AI drafts, the coach decides: AI produces the plan and the coach reviews, edits or replaces it

    I chose the third. A plan generates weekly from the trainee's goal, which removes the blank page. Coaches stay the final authority because injury risk, recovery and performance calls need someone who sees the trainee's real data and context. It also protects the coach's role, since this is a coach-led platform and every coach has their own training style.

    What coaches can do

    • Start from a weekly AI draft built from the trainee's goal
    • See past weeks of real run data beside the plan: heart rate, pace, distance and time, synced from the trainee's watch (Garmin, Apple Watch, Samsung Health, COROS)
    • Decide what to change, for example lowering intensity when a trainee isn't keeping up or when feedback or heart rate points that way
    • Approve before the plan reaches the trainee, edit on any day, or build a plan from scratch

    Tradeoffs

    • Full automation versus what's achievable now. The fuller vision has AI analyzing trainee feedback, summarizing it and adjusting plans automatically, with the coach approving. It needs significant model training and data, so I shipped the weekly draft with the coach doing the review and judgment. The cost: for now, coaches make the adjustments themselves.
    • Control versus speed. Review adds work, which is the main adoption risk. Run data sits next to the plan to keep decisions quick.
    • Editing any day versus a weekly cycle. Real training changes mid-week, and a rigid cycle would push coaches back to WhatsApp.
    • Flexibility versus structure. Coaches can modify the AI plan or build their own, because one method would have lost the experienced ones.

    How I'll know it's working

    The North Star is trainees with a coach-managed plan each week, meaning their coach reviewed, adjusted or approved the plan that week.

    • Inputs: active coaches using the plan flow weekly, share of trainees whose plan was touched that week, time a coach spends on a review, and which parameters coaches change most
    • Quality signals: plan modification rate (a signal, not a target) and zero-edit approval rate, to catch rubber-stamping
    • Guardrails: plans reaching trainees without coach review, trainee plan adherence, coach churn

    First-phase targets, set as hypotheses to test:

    • 70% of onboarded coaches using the plan flow every week within 60 days
    • 80% of trainees' plans reviewed or adjusted by their coach at least weekly
    • Plan review under 10 minutes on average
    • Zero-edit approvals below roughly 70%, so reviews stay real
    • 80% of coaches still active four weeks after onboarding

    Outcome

    ~50 coaches onboarded to the coach app. The plan review and edit flow is live, and I'm tracking the North Star and these metrics against the targets.

    What's next (planned, not built)

    • AI analysis of trainee feedback into a coach-ready summary
    • Plans that adjust automatically, with the coach still approving
    • Coach-made plans saved as templates
    • Pattern recognition from those plans, so a plan can readjust after 5 to 6 weeks, with coaches still in the loop

    Nutrition for runners: choosing the one MVP worth building first

    Run-linked fuelling for a coach-led running platform: what to build first, and why.

    At a glanceStatus: MVP defined, not yet shipped
    Scope: competitor research, feature ideation and prioritization
    My role: Assistant Product Manager. I led ideation and prioritization for the module.

    The context

    RunPlayGo provides AI-powered training plans and nutrition for runners, with coaches in the loop. Nutrition is the module I scoped: what it should do first, and how it connects to the coach's plan.

    The problem: runners need fuel planned around training, not a calorie counter

    Most nutrition apps count calories. A runner needs to know what to eat around a session, and their coach needs to see it too. The competitor review covered four products, and each solves one piece well:

    • Fuelin and Hexis set each meal's carb level from the day's training session, but they are built for global endurance athletes with no Indian food focus
    • Precision Fuel & Hydration owns sweat rate and race fuelling
    • HealthifyMe leads in India on fast AI logging of Indian food, but it is built for weight loss, not training

    None of them lets a runner's own coach see the same data. That was the gap: run-linked fuelling, Indian food, and a coach who sees both.

    The decision: what to build first

    The brainstorm produced ideas across 15 areas, from logging and race plans to labs and a nutritionist marketplace. I weighed three directions:

    • A calorie and macro tracker: familiar, but it competes head on with HealthifyMe
    • A full athlete nutrition suite: race plans, sweat tests, labs and nutritionists. It is the highest value but needs the most legal review, consent and supply
    • Run-linked fuelling first: the coach's plan drives what a runner should eat, with the expert layer added once daily logging sticks

    I chose the third. It is what makes the product different from HealthifyMe on day one, and it ties nutrition to the coach's plan.

    What the MVP does

    • A diet profile and a daily carb level set by the coach's plan
    • A day timeline with meals placed around the run
    • An AI meal scanner for photo, voice and search logging, built for Indian and sports foods
    • An AI meal score against today's run, with one reason and one swap
    • Hydration and macros, plus a fuel summary the coach can see

    Tradeoffs

    • Run-linked fuelling versus a broad feature set. Race plans, sweat tests and supplements wait for a later release. The cost: v1 is narrower than the competitors' full apps.
    • Fast logging versus accuracy on Indian food. Mixed dishes and portions are hard to read from a photo, so correcting the AI has to be quick, and I'd track how often users correct it.
    • Guidance versus liability. Carb, protein and fluid thresholds and supplement advice need a sports nutritionist's sign-off, and diabetic runners need clinician-set plans.
    • Experts now versus later. Labs and a nutritionist marketplace need explicit consent, legal review under India's DPDP Act and nutritionist supply, so they come last.

    How I'll know it's working

    These are proposed measures to validate once the MVP is in testing. The North Star is the share of training days where the runner logs or confirms their fuel.

    • Inputs: runners who log a meal in their first week, share of scans accepted without correction, meal score views, and how often coaches open the fuel summary
    • Guardrails: AI correction rate, logging drop-off after the first week, and any guidance flagged in the nutritionist review

    Status: MVP defined, not yet shipped.

    What's next, and what's still open

    • v2, recover and race: morning check-in, sleep and watch sync, a sweat test and a race nutrition planner
    • v3, expert: a runner lab panel, a nutritionist marketplace and condition-aware plans
    • Still open: which runner segment comes first, whether the paid tier is about performance or health, how nutritionists are sourced, and whether nutrition lives on one Today view or its own tab

    PhonePe: making a failed payment a moment of trust

    An independent product teardown based on public information. My analysis, not shipped work.

    At a glanceProduct: PhonePe, consumer UPI app
    Focus: failed and pending payments
    My role: problem selection, solution design, metrics and tradeoffs

    Where PhonePe stands

    PhonePe handles nearly half of all UPI transactions and has expanded into lending and insurance distribution, Share.Market and the Indus Appstore. Two signals shaped this teardown.

    • AI is already on the surface. In February 2026 it launched natural-language search that lets users pay or get help by voice or text, replacing menu navigation with intent-based routing.
    • The market is more contested. PhonePe and Google Pay's combined UPI share fell below 80% for the first time in May 2026, and users can keep more than one payments app, so switching costs are low.

    The insight

    In a market where users switch apps freely, a payments app is judged less on the thousands of payments that work and more on the one that doesn't. When money has left an account and the user can't tell where it is, trust is made or lost, and it's the moment most apps explain least.

    Who it's for, and which problem to solve

    The everyday UPI payer: someone paying daily for groceries, bills, rent and transfers, often with more than one payments app. Their job is simple: pay reliably, and always know where their money is. I weighed four problems:

    • Failed or pending payments: the user doesn't know what happens next
    • Fraud and scams: real, but PhonePe has already launched PhonePe Protect to guard against fraudulent transactions
    • App complexity as services expand: real, but it touches fewer daily moments
    • Spend insights: useful, but not tied to the core job

    I chose the first. It sits on the core job, it creates the highest anxiety, and trust at that moment is what keeps a user from opening a competitor. To validate it, I'd start the way I did with the coach app: talk to frequent UPI users about the last time a payment failed or hung, what they did and what they wished the app had told them.

    The decision: AI explains, the system resolves

    I weighed three options:

    • Fully automated: AI decides and acts on stuck payments, including refunds
    • Fully manual: every issue goes to support
    • AI explains, the system and humans resolve: AI says what is happening and what comes next, while the payment rails, banks and support stay the authority on the money

    I chose the third, the same principle as my coach app: AI proposes and a person with context decides. Money movement involves banks and the network, which the app doesn't control, and a wrong AI action on someone's money costs far more than a slow one.

    The proposal: a payment status and resolution layer

    When a payment fails or is pending, the user sees:

    • A plain-language status of what happened and what happens next, in their language
    • An expected resolution window shown as a range based on typical bank and network timelines, not a promise
    • A proactive update when the payment resolves or the refund lands
    • An AI explanation from the transaction history, so they can ask why it is pending and get an answer based on the transaction's actual state
    • One-tap escalation with the details pre-filled if the window passes without resolution

    This builds on the AI search PhonePe already launched, moving it from finding and routing to explaining and resolving.

    Tradeoffs

    • Transparency versus control. Show ranges and states, not guarantees: PhonePe doesn't control every step, and a missed promise damages trust more than an honest range. The cost is less reassurance than a firm deadline.
    • AI explanation versus accuracy. The assistant explains from the structured transaction state, not free text, and hands off to a human when unsure. The cost is narrower answers at first.
    • Full coverage versus a focused start. Begin with the most common failure types and the largest banks, since a reliable narrow version beats a patchy broad one.
    • Reliability versus new services. Every other service sits on the payments base, so protecting it matters more than adding surface area. The cost is fewer visible launches in the short term.

    How I'd measure it

    The North Star is the share of failed or pending transactions where the user reaches a clear resolution without contacting support.

    • Inputs: support contacts per 1,000 failed or pending transactions, status screen opens, and how often the assistant answers without escalation
    • Retention signal: how often users who hit a failure keep paying on PhonePe, compared with similar users who didn't
    • Guardrails: duplicate payments (users retrying because they're unsure), escalation volume, wrong AI explanations, and any gap between shown and actual resolution times

    What I'd do first

    • Interview frequent UPI users about their last failed payment
    • Map the current in-app flow for failed and pending states and find the exact gaps
    • Review the main failure types by frequency and severity
    • Prototype the status screen and test comprehension before building the AI layer

    Spotify: let free listeners try the AI that makes Premium worth paying for

    An independent product teardown based on public information. My analysis, not shipped work.

    At a glanceProduct: Spotify, consumer app
    Focus: free listeners and AI personalization
    My role: problem selection, solution design, metrics and tradeoffs

    Where Spotify stands

    In Q2 2026 Spotify reported 777 million monthly active users and 300 million Premium subscribers, which leaves about 494 million on the ad-supported tier. Its newest AI feature, Prompted Playlist, lets listeners describe what they want in their own words, edit or restart the prompt, set the playlist to refresh daily or weekly, and see a short reason for each track. So far it has rolled out to Premium users, in English, in its early markets.

    The gap

    Spotify has built a strong personalization engine, but its newest part is so far reserved for Premium. The free listener, the biggest group, gets recommendations but can't steer them, and can't see what Premium would actually change for them.

    Who it's for, and which problem to solve

    The regular free listener: someone who streams often, has real taste and hasn't upgraded. The problems, in order of impact:

    • No way to steer. Recommendations come to them, but they can't say what they want right now
    • No way to see Premium's value. Nothing shows what upgrading would change
    • The blank prompt. People have to know what to type, which is why Spotify added an Ideas option
    • English first. Early AI features in one language limit reach in multilingual markets

    To validate it, I'd talk to regular free listeners about the last time they wanted music for a specific moment, what they did and what they wished the app had done.

    The decision: AI drafts, the listener steers

    I weighed three options:

    • AI decides everything: it picks and plays, and the listener accepts
    • Listener builds everything: manual playlists with no AI help
    • AI drafts, the listener steers: AI builds the playlist from the prompt and listening history, and the listener edits it and sees why each track was chosen

    I chose the third, the same principle as my coach app: AI does the heavy lifting and the person with the context makes the call. Music is personal, so a listener who can see the reasoning and change the result will trust the product more than one who is handed an answer.

    The proposal: a metered Prompted Playlist for free listeners

    • A small allowance of prompted playlists for free users, built from their own listening history
    • The why-it-fits line stays on every track, because it builds trust
    • Same quality as Premium: limit how many a free user gets, not how good the first one is
    • A natural upgrade moment after a good result, since a playlist that refreshes on its own and unlimited prompts stay with Premium
    • Start in markets where the feature already runs, then expand

    Tradeoffs

    • Free taste versus Premium value. A limited taste shows the value without removing the reason to pay. The cost is that some users will be satisfied with one playlist and never upgrade.
    • AI cost versus reach. Cap the allowance and use a lower-cost generation path for free usage, without cutting the quality of the first result, because a poor first impression wastes the trial. The cost is that some users will hit the cap.
    • Conversion versus trust. Show the upgrade offer only after the listener has received value, and never put the result behind it. The cost is lower short-term conversion.
    • Heavy listeners first. More history means a better first result, so I'd start with regular listeners. The cost is slower reach.
    • What's feasible now versus the full vision. The vision is Spotify spotting a listener's recurring moments and suggesting the playlist before they type. That needs a lot of behavior data and tuning, so I'd ship the metered prompt first.

    How I'd measure it

    The North Star is the 30-day Premium conversion rate of free users offered the trial, compared with a holdout group that wasn't.

    • Inputs: share who see the entry point, create a prompt, play the result past a few tracks, hit the allowance limit, or tap the upgrade offer
    • Quality signals: skips and early abandonment on generated playlists, and how often listeners edit the prompt
    • Guardrails: Premium churn and Premium use of the feature (to catch cannibalization), AI cost per free user, and ad-supported listening

    I'd ship wider only if conversion lifts against the holdout, Premium churn doesn't rise, and AI cost per free user stays below what the extra conversions earn.

    What's next (planned, not built)

    • Save prompts that worked as reusable templates, like a focus or gym playlist
    • Notice when a listener repeats a moment and suggest the playlist before they type, with the listener approving
    • Expand to more languages

    What I'd do first

    • Use the free and Premium tiers hands-on and map what a free user sees today
    • Interview regular free listeners about why they haven't upgraded
    • Estimate the cost per prompt and the break-even conversion lift
    • Prototype the metered trial and run it as an A/B test with a holdout in one live market

    How I work

    Questions I get asked, answered from the work above.

    How do you decide what AI does and what a person decides?

    I start with what a wrong answer would cost. On the coach app, injury risk and recovery calls need someone who sees the trainee's real data, so AI drafts the weekly plan and the coach reviews, changes and approves it. The same rule shaped my PhonePe and Spotify teardowns: AI explains or suggests, and the system or the user keeps the final say.

    How do you start on a problem?

    With the people who live with it. Before designing the coach app I spoke with up to 30 coaches about how they plan, track trainees and communicate. Coaches had no platform or tools, and everything was scattered across WhatsApp, Excel and notebooks.

    How do you prioritize when time is short?

    I shipped Coach Plan Modification in 30 days by focusing on the review loop where the coach makes the judgment. I held back the fuller version, where AI analyzes feedback and adjusts plans itself, because it needs far more model training and data. I also built coach supply before trainee acquisition, since this is a coach-led platform.

    How do you measure success?

    With a North Star, input metrics and guardrails. For plan modification the North Star is trainees with a coach-managed plan each week, and a guardrail watches the zero-edit approval rate so review doesn't turn into rubber-stamping. The targets I set are hypotheses to test.
